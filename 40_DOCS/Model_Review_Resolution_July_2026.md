\# Model Review: How Each Stage Was Resolved \*\*July 2026. Companion to the Project Status Report.\*\* ## What This Document Is An internal review of the player valuation model raised thirty-odd items across five stages. This records what I did about each one, in plain language, with the numbers that decided it. Where I rejected a recommendation, the reason is here. Where I accepted one and it turned out smaller or larger than the review expected, that is here too. Three things run through the whole exercise and are worth stating once at the top. \*\*I fixed the reading rule before running the test, each time it mattered.\*\* For the two decisions that could have moved a headline finding, I wrote down in advance what result would count as which answer, and signed it before the code was written. This is the only reason the results are worth anything: the finding that came back is the one that suits my argument, and I need to be able to show that I could not have engineered it. \*\*Several corrections point in opposite directions.\*\* Four separate changes now sit in the model. Two raise valuations and two lower them. On eight-year contracts they net to a 1.5 percent change. That is a stronger robustness statement than making no changes at all. \*\*A few things came back smaller than advertised.\*\* The review estimated one correction at 20 to 25 percent and it came in at 12. Another rested on a premise a different test had already destroyed. I have said so in each case rather than quoting the review's figures. --- ## Stage 1: Corrections That Required No Decision These were mechanical errors: a lookup indexed one position off, a missing lower bound, risk cells reading zero and being treated as certainty. They were resolved before the work recorded here and their effects show in the validation logs. Two are worth naming because they moved real money. \*\*The survival lookup was off by one year.\*\* The table that estimates the chance a player is still playing was being read at the wrong age and quality position. Correcting it moved 921 of 2,909 contracts, 479 up and 442 down, for an aggregate shift of $136.5 million. A thirty-year-old fringe player on a five-year deal had a 33.5 percent chance of still playing in the fifth season under the old lookup and 45.5 percent under the corrected one. \*\*The projection had a guard on one side only.\*\* Sixty-seven contract-seasons across thirty-nine contracts wanted a negative multiplier, meaning the model was predicting production in the opposite direction to the player's own baseline. Each now sits at replacement level. --- ## Stage 2: Does Contract Length Carry Real Value? ### The question Long contracts cost more per season, even after accounting for how good the player is. Adding contract length to the price equation moves the price of one win from $1.84 million to $0.99 million and lifts the share of price the equation explains from 46.5 percent to 72.7 percent. Length is worth $0.82 million per additional year, and the statistical strength of that is overwhelming. Two explanations were available. Either teams know something about the player that the model does not, so a long deal signals real future performance and the model should credit it. Or teams simply pay too much for long deals, in which case the model's large negative values on long contracts are a real finding rather than a bug. This gated everything else. If length carries genuine value, the price equation had to change and every downstream component with it. ### What I did I wrote a specification and signed it before any estimation was run. The design fixes the sample, the outcome measure, the equation, and the rule for reading the answer. Nothing in it was revised afterwards. Two design points in that specification matter more than the rest. \*\*I changed what gets measured.\*\* The review proposed testing realized production "over the life of the contract." That quietly requires the contract to have finished. Since the game data runs through 2025-26 and contracts start in 2018, only 5 of 93 eight-year contracts have completed. Worse, whether a contract has finished is determined by its length, which is the variable under test, so the sample would have been stripped of exactly the contracts that matter. I measured production over a fixed three seasons after signing instead, whether or not the contract was still running. That keeps 64 eight-year contracts instead of 5. \*\*I refused to run a preliminary version.\*\* I could have run the test using Bacon WAR as the outcome, since that data was on hand. Using the same source that produced the projection could never settle the question, and having seen a same-source answer I could no longer claim the test was specified blind. The outcome measure is Game Value, the game-level metric built from raw league play-by-play, which contains no Bacon input at all. ### The answer Contract length buys no additional realized production. The coefficient is −0.0135 wins per season per year of term, with a 95 percent interval running from −0.036 to +0.009. The share of the length premium attributable to correct private information is zero. \*\*The important part is that this is a confident no, not a shrug.\*\* For the length premium to be fully justified, players would have needed to beat expectations by about 0.83 wins per season for each extra contract year. The very best the data allows is 0.009, which is roughly one percent of what would be required. A test that merely lacked power would have produced a wide, useless interval. This one is tight and sits on zero. ### What follows Contract length is not credited on the value side. The proposed correction, which would have valued every player at a common contract length, is not adopted. The negative values on long contracts stand, and they now have a pre-registered test behind them rather than an unexamined omission. \*\*One limit on the claim.\*\* This shows that term does not buy production. It does not show that term is worthless. A club signing eight years also locks in a price and avoids bidding for that player again, and the metric cannot see that. The honest sentence is that term does not buy production. \*\*Two cautions.\*\* Splitting the sample, forwards came out slightly positive and free-agent signings slightly negative, both tiny, and eight splits were run so one or two odd results are expected. And the two metrics may not sit on quite the same scale; correcting for that as generously as possible leaves the conclusion intact. --- ## Stage 3: What the Price Equation Should Be ### The floor problem, and why it mattered most There is a legal minimum salary, $775,000 in 2025-26. In the sample of 2,349 contracts, \*\*529 pay exactly that, to the dollar.\*\* Those numbers are not opinions about players. A club that thinks a fourth-liner is worth $300,000 cannot offer $300,000, so the contract reads $775,000. So does the contract of a player the club values at $600,000. Three different views, one recorded number. Fitting a line through those as if they were negotiated prices drags the bottom of the line upward. That is why the model said a zero-win player was worth $1.75 million, more than twice the minimum a club can actually pay. The fix is a censored regression. For contracts above the minimum, use the salary as written. For the 529 at the minimum, tell the model only that the value was at or below the floor, and nothing more. The word "censoring" is statistical language for data cut off at a limit, in the way a thermometer that cannot read below zero records every cold day as zero. \*\*This mattered more than any other single change\*\*, because the review showed that a meaningful share of a later calibration step existed only to repair damage the uncensored price equation had created. ### Does the price of a win bend? The review found that one win does not cost the same whoever earns it, and proposed letting the price bend at production levels of 0, 1, and 2 wins. Refitting inside the censored model reproduced the review's shape closely, including the flattening above two wins that the collective agreement's cap on individual contracts produces. In-sample the evidence looked overwhelming. The formal test returned a chi-squared of 160.6 and the conservative model-selection criterion preferred the bent version by 137 points. \*\*Then I tested it out of sample and it failed.\*\* Fitting on contracts signed 2018 to 2022 and predicting those signed 2023 to 2025, the bent version predicted \*\*worse\*\* than a straight line: an error of $1.9663 million against $1.9512 million, with the paired comparison at p = 0.33 across 635 contracts. Both conditions of the rule I had fixed in advance failed, so the bend is not adopted. Two supporting findings confirmed it. Moving the lowest break point off zero swung the implied value of a replacement player from −$1.45 million to +$1.19 million, which is not a parameter the data can pin down. And the shape was unstable across halves of the sample: the middle segment moved by $640,000 per win and two segments swapped order. \*\*The lesson is worth carrying.\*\* A more flexible model always fits its own data better. Only held-out prediction distinguishes structure from noise. ### Position Defencemen are paid more than forwards at the same measured production, by about $739,000 per season, and the evidence is overwhelming. But the model's errors also do not average to zero within position, which matters because the back-test slices trades by position and would manufacture apparent mispricing on every forward-for-defenceman deal. The first version I tried added a flat premium for defencemen. Checking against the data showed that was the wrong shape: \*\*at zero measured wins, forwards and defencemen are paid the same\*\*, a median of $0.886 million each. A flat premium asserts a gap where none exists. So position enters as a difference in slope with a common intercept. A defenceman's win prices at $2.30 million against a forward's $2.03 million, and at zero wins the two are identical. That collapses the positional error gap from $622,000 to $80,000 with the flat version, or to $496,000 with the slope version, and I took the slope version on the grounds that a model should not contain a claim the data contradicts. The residual gap is carried as a stated limitation. \*\*What this number means is genuinely unresolved.\*\* Either clubs overpay defencemen, or the production measure does not capture what defencemen do. My own validation work found the measure tracks an independent benchmark at about 0.65 for forwards and 0.28 to 0.30 for defencemen, so the second reading is at least as likely. One piece of evidence leans that way: with a flat premium, the model implies a zero-win defenceman is worth $1.73 million when a club can sign a body for $775,000. The model is left agnostic and the premium is reported as a two-sided finding rather than as mispricing. ### A benchmark I got wrong I initially judged the specifications by how close the value of a replacement player came to the league minimum. That was wrong, and the correction came from outside the review. Replacement level in this model is the marginal roster player, the thirteenth forward and the seventh defenceman, and not the cheapest signable body. Measuring what those players actually earn, by ice-time rank within each team-season: \*\*a thirteenth forward earns a median of $0.984 million and only 30 percent of them are on the minimum at all.\*\* The model's figure of $0.977 million lands within $7,000 of that. The corrected benchmark is better than the one it replaced, and it needs no opinion about what a replacement player ought to cost. Because an observed price is the higher of the true price and the floor, the observed average price of zero-win players, $1.336 million, is a \*\*ceiling\*\* on what the censored model can legitimately imply. Applying that eliminated the length-controlled specification outright, since it implies $1.818 million. ### Pricing a range rather than a single guess The model takes one number for each future season, its best guess of production, and runs it through the price equation. The future is a range. With a straight-line price equation this makes no difference at all: the value of the average outcome equals the average of the outcome values. The one exception is the league-minimum floor, which caps the downside and leaves the upside open, so for a player near the floor the average of the outcomes is worth more than the single best guess. The review measured this against the bent curve and reported figures roughly twice as large, including negative corrections for good players. On a straight line the correction is positive everywhere and dies out above about 1.5 wins. The final figure is \*\*$147,000 per contract\*\*, more than double the $68,200 measured before the price equation was corrected, because the lower intercept tripled the number of seasons sitting at the floor. One-year contracts receive exactly zero, since the valuation season uses observed production rather than a projection. ### The rate now in force Value equals \*\*1.32 percent of the cap, plus 2.12 percent for each win by a forward or 2.41 percent for each win by a defenceman.\*\* At the 2025-26 ceiling that is $1.27 million plus $2.03 million or $2.30 million per win. I also checked whether restricted and unrestricted free agents need separate lines. They do not, under the censored fit as under the original one. --- ## Stage 4: Everything the Price Equation Feeds ### The retention calibration was fitted on the wrong sample When a restricted free agent's contract ends, the club decides whether to tender him. The model uses observed retention rates rather than the general exit-risk figure, and the reasoning is sound: declining to tender is itself a departure, so using both would charge the same risk twice. \*\*The rates were fitted on a sample that excluded the departures.\*\* Sorting a player into a talent bucket needs a production figure, and a player with no recent NHL games has none, so he was skipped. Players with no recent NHL games are exactly the ones clubs walk away from. | group | decisions | walked away from ||---|---|---|| used in the calibration | 1,231 | 21.3% || dropped for no recent games | 1,039 | 33.9% || all eligible | 2,270 | 27.0% | Nearly half the decisions were dropped, and the dropped group was walked away from 1.6 times as often. The logic was circular: exit risk was switched off because the retention rates covered departure, and the rates were fitted without the departures. The fix treats a missing production figure as informative rather than missing. A player who could not hold an NHL job belongs in the below-replacement bucket. The overall walk-away rate moves from 21.3 percent to 27.0 percent, and the star, regular, and fringe buckets do not move at all, which is the check that confirms the fix landed where it should. \*\*It is smaller than the review claimed.\*\* The review estimated terminal value was overstated by 20 to 25 percent for a player with three control years. The actual figure is 12.3 percent; five control years are needed to reach 20. The reason is that most of the headline gap is composition, more decisions sitting in the low bucket, rather than any bucket's rate moving much, and composition does not compound the way a rate does. ### Is the calibration now over-correcting? Three things now push fringe valuations down at once: the lower intercept, the retention gate, and the gate made more aggressive. Each is defensible alone, and stacked they might overshoot. Tested against the corrected price equation, the model still implies 91.6 percent retention for fringe players against an observed 79.3 percent, and 79.6 against 67.3 for below-replacement players. The model remains more generous than clubs are, so the gate is still doing real work and no change is needed. The price-equation fix did remove about half the original problem, exactly as the review predicted: the over-generosity in the below-replacement bucket fell from 23.3 points to 12.3. \*\*One caveat belongs in the write-up.\*\* The verdict rests on a margin of about $300,000 between what the model says a zero-win player is worth and a typical qualifying offer. At the low end of the intercept's own defensible range, the answer reverses. ### Where the floor sits, and a recommendation I withdrew The review argued that the minimum-salary floor should be applied after the survival adjustment rather than before it, on the reasoning that a roster spot holds minimum value whether or not the original player is there. \*\*I recommended accepting this and then withdrew it\*\*, because the objection to it is better than the argument for it. We are valuing a player, not a roster spot. If a club employs him, it has revealed it thinks he is worth at least what a replacement would give, and that justifies the floor \*\*conditional on him being employed\*\*. Survival is the probability of that condition holding, so the floor is correctly risked with everything else. There is a second reason. Crediting the floor unconditionally would book replacement-player value without booking replacement-player cost. When a player disappears the club does not receive $775,000; it has to go and sign somebody and pay him. Since the cost side is deliberately unconditional, adding unconditional floor value on the other side is one-sided. No change to the calculation. What changes is the written justification, which matters, because a reader following the review's reasoning would reach the opposite conclusion. ### The draft curve was not independent of the price equation The curve reads as finished work and is not. Every surplus figure in it runs through the market rate, so it was stale the moment the rate changed. Refitting on the corrected rate moved it substantially and unevenly: | bucket | before | after | change ||---|---|---|---|| pick 1 | $13.73M | $13.65M | −0.6% || picks 6-10 | $6.69M | $5.75M | −14.1% || picks 21-32 | $4.21M | $3.20M | −24.0% || picks 151-224 | $0.81M | $0.61M | −25.0% | \*\*The curve steepened sharply.\*\* The ratio between the first pick and the last bucket went from 16.9 to 22.4. The reason is a break-even. The intercept fell more than the slopes rose, so a player only gains under the new rate if he produced above 2.56 wins per 82 games as a forward, or 1.04 as a defenceman. Almost no entry-level player clears 2.56, so only 56 of 2,091 skaters moved up. Those 56 have a median draft position of 24. The first pick held because its eleven-player cohort contains most of the entry-level players who clear the bar. \*\*This bears on a headline.\*\* The overpayment ratio for top-ten picks was computed against a curve that no longer exists, and has to be recomputed before it is quoted. ### The rule I kept, and why The curve values entry-level seasons only, treating everything after that as zero surplus on the reasoning that players past their entry-level deal are paid the going rate. That rule is measurably not neutral. Measured against the model's own output across 547 second contracts signed by drafted players, mean value runs from −$19.70 million at the first pick to +$1.45 million past pick 100, crossing zero near pick 30. So the rule flatters the top of the draft. The review predicted the opposite sign, on the premise that contract length carries market value. Stage 2 destroyed that premise. I kept the rule anyway, deliberately. Whatever replaces it will be superseded again once the draft and prospect pillars are built, because those will supply prior information about entry-level players that the curve currently has no way to use. Rebuilding it twice is wasted work. It is provisional, not settled, and the measured bias is recorded. --- ## Stage 5: Identification and Documentation \*\*Three components use the whole sample to value the past.\*\* The aging curve, the price equation, and the retirement-risk table are each fitted on 2018 to 2025 and applied backwards, so a valuation dated 2018 draws partly on information from 2024. The defence is that these are structural market parameters rather than player-specific information. Only the aging curve was flagged; all three are now stated together. The stability check has been run for the price equation and it passes comfortably. Fitted on 2018 to 2021 alone, the value of a replacement player moves by −3.0 percent, the forward rate by +1.5 percent, and the defence rate by +2.9 percent. A two-win valuation moves by $32,000 out of $5.3 million. \*\*This holds despite the cap rising from $79.5 million to $95.5 million across the window\*\*, which is evidence that working in cap share rather than dollars is doing its job. The same check for the aging curve and the risk table is outstanding. \*\*One documented claim was not true.\*\* The aging curve's documentation says no look-ahead is present and each input is trailing. That holds for the player being projected, whose own future seasons are excluded, and not for his comparables, whose profiles come from the whole panel. The wording is corrected. \*\*The discount structure.\*\* The specification defines the discount rate as injury risk plus the opportunity cost of cap space. Injury risk is present. The 3 percent figure is cap growth, which converts dollars across years and does not charge for tying up cap space. I have rewritten the specification to state that expressing value in cap-share terms is the treatment of cap opportunity cost, rather than adding a separate charge that would double-count against the cap-share framing. The limitation this leaves is that the model does not price the optionality forgone by committing cap space over long horizons. Adding that charge would make long contracts look worse, so its absence does not flatter the long-contract finding. \*\*Replacement-reversion and the risk tiers.\*\* Reverting a below-replacement player toward zero puts him in a lower risk tier than his observed production implies. I kept it, because below-replacement players recover 93 percent of the time, which makes the reverted figure the honest expectation. The bias worth recording is that the 93 percent is conditional on the player appearing again, and exit is precisely the event that removes him from that sample, so exit risk is probably understated for these players. \*\*The draft curve now carries bootstrap intervals\*\* instead of standard errors that assumed a symmetry the data does not have. The first pick is $13.65 million with an interval from $9.24 million to $18.61 million on eleven observations. The gap between the first and second pick is $5.29 million with an interval from $0.18 million to $10.82 million, and a 2.0 percent chance the ordering reverses on resampling. No claim that the first pick is worth substantially more than the second should appear without that interval attached. \*\*Name matching.\*\* 16.3 percent of the curve's value rests on picks resolved by name or alias rather than a shared identifier: 283 name matches carrying 15.0 percent and 16 alias matches carrying 1.3 percent. No incorrect match has been found. The exposure is stated rather than left implicit. --- ## What Is Carried Forward \*\*Open items:\*\* - The stability check for the aging curve and the retirement-risk table- Whether the model should price the cost certainty a long contract buys, which the term test cannot see- The overpayment ratio for top-ten picks, which needs recomputing on the steepened curve- Goaltenders, left on the older least-squares rate until the goalie pillar's own items are settled\*\*Recorded biases, each with its direction stated:\*\* - Zero surplus after the entry-level contract flatters the top of the draft- Exit risk is understated for below-replacement players- Cap optionality is not priced, which does not flatter long contracts- Positional error of about $496,000 per season remains after the position term- 44.6 percent of restricted-free-agent contracts have no salary data, so the qualifying offer falls back to average annual value\*\*One check I deliberately skipped\*\*, recorded so it does not read as an oversight: the two raw variants of the Game Value metric were pre-specified as robustness legs for the term test and were not run, on the judgement that the documented agreement between the raw and adjusted versions made a different answer unlikely. ## What Changed, in One Table | | before the review | after ||---|---|---|| value of a zero-win player | $1.749M | $1.265M || price of a win, forwards | $1.838M | $2.028M || price of a win, defencemen | $1.838M | $2.302M || fitting method | least squares | censored at the league minimum || minimum-salary floor binds on | 5.5% of seasons | 15.3% || retention walk-away rate | 21.3% | 27.0% || draft curve, pick 1 | $13.73M | $13.65M || draft curve, picks 151-224 | $0.81M | $0.61M || median contract value | +$0.78M | +$0.32M || eight-year contracts, mean | −$26.74M | −$26.35M | The last line is the one to keep in view.
