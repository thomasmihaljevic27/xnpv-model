## The interval layer

The harness has scored interval coverage since the day it was written and the column has been
empty in every single run, because no model has ever stated a range. That is the gap.

The distribution is a mixture rather than a bell curve, and the reason is the point of the whole
thing: a season's win total has a lump of probability sitting on exactly zero, for the seasons a
player spends out of the league, and a continuous spread for the seasons he plays. For a fringe
player who is even money to play at all, the honest tenth percentile *is* zero, and no curve
centred on 0.4 wins will say so. The lump sits in the middle of the spread rather than at its
edge, because a player who does play can still finish below zero wins, so the quantile function
has a flat step at zero and the rows whose stated percentile lands inside it get zero as their
answer. That is why the inversion is written out by hand.

The spread is fitted rolling, by replaying the model on pages whose outcome seasons had all
finished before the decision date. Scale is a straight line in the size of the forecast, fitted
per season ahead and floored so that a player forecast at nothing still gets a band. Shape is
the empirical distribution of the scaled misses, pooled across horizons, which is what carries
the long right tail and short left tail that no two-parameter curve would.

It is a wrapper, not a subclass, so every existing candidate and every future one gets a band
without a line of its own changing — and so the point forecast provably cannot move.

## How it was checked, given that the model here is crippled

Three ways, none of which depends on the model being any good.

The arithmetic is checked against a case whose answer is known by construction: seasons drawn
from a mixture the test chose, the same parameters handed to the quantile function, the stated
ends compared against the simulated ones. Five player types at three levels, including the
fringe player and a negative forecast. They agree to within Monte Carlo error everywhere.

The band collapses onto the point forecast when the spread is set to nothing — exactly zero gap
across five quantiles and five thousand rows. That is the identity the plan asks the simulation
to satisfy, tested one layer down where it is cheap.

The wrapper moves nothing: 40,510 scored rows, largest change to any point forecast exactly
zero.

## The leakage battery

The existing stress suite checks look-ahead once, on two pages, at two horizons, for one of the
three quantities a model produces. Five tests now, four about leakage.

The new one that matters hands the model the **entire** source, 2007 to 2025, and tells it only
which year it is standing in. The old test hands it a table the harness has already filtered and
then filters it again, so it can only catch a model reaching for data it was never given. This
one relies on the model's own rule — that a training pair counts only if its outcome season
finished before the decision date. A model that filtered its inputs and not its outcomes passes
the old test and fails this one. It is not a hypothetical: a pair whose inputs end three seasons
back still has an outcome landing next year. And it will be load-bearing, because the trade-date
valuation builds its own information sets rather than taking the harness's.

Scrambling the future in place rather than deleting it keeps the table's shape, so a path that
short-circuits on an empty frame cannot pass by accident. Pairing every forecast with a different
player's season checks the scoring join itself.

All four pass. Largest change in the first three: 0.00e+00, every page, every horizon, the rate,
the games share, the participation probability and both ends of the band. The placebo raises the
error 29 to 39%.

The fifth test is not about leakage. It perturbs an input the model is entitled to see and
measures the response, which puts one number on the shrinkage the rebuild rests on: about 30% of
a one-season shock survives into the forecast.

## What the band is worth, now that the model is the real one

The aggregate is good. The 80% band holds 82 to 84% of seasons at every horizon and the 90% band
holds 91%, both erring slightly wide, which is the cheaper direction. The 50% band over-covers at
long horizons for a structural reason rather than a defect: enough players have a real chance of
not playing that the lump on zero swallows the middle half, and a band with both ends on zero
necessarily holds more than half the outcomes.

Pooling the shape of a miss **across horizons** survives its check — the 5th and 95th move from
−1.85/+2.46 at the valuation season to −1.55/+2.75 nine seasons out, around a pooled −1.72/+2.56.
That was the assumption most likely to be wrong.

**Pooling it across tiers and ages does not, and it fails exactly where this project is already
weakest.** At the stated 80%, the 3+ tier runs 0.852 at the valuation season down to **0.596**
five seasons out. The 22-and-unders run 0.754 to 0.663. The 34-and-overs go the other way, to
0.977. A band that claims 80% and delivers 60% for the players who carry the money is not a
detail.

## The part worth being careful about

A band can miss too often for two reasons and the repair is opposite in the two cases, so the
runner now separates them: divide each miss by the band it was given, and report the middle and
the ends apart. A shifted middle is the forecast being wrong. A stretched 5th-to-95th is the band
being wrong.

**Most of the star under-coverage is the forecast.** The middle of the star misses at five
seasons out sits at +0.44 where the band expects −0.11. That is the star residual already on the
queue, now showing up a second way. A correctly sized band around a centre that is too low misses
high, and **widening the band would hide a known bias behind a bigger interval.** That repair was
available, would have made the coverage table look much better, and was not made.

**Some of it is the band.** The star and young-player right tails reach +3.41 and +3.65 against a
pooled +2.56, while their left tails sit where everyone else's do. So the shape genuinely is not
one shape across tiers and ages. But fitting a fatter tail around a biased centre is fitting the
wrong thing, so the order is: fix the centre, then re-measure.

The star residual is therefore promoted in the queue. It now blocks two things.

## Two other numbers

## The implementation review, and three things it caught

Astra reviewed `73b77ee`, reproduced the coverage tables, the export guard, all 21 repair checks
with the production workbook, and the three zero-change leakage results. Three findings, all
correct, all fixed. One of them reverses something this session published, and one of them is the
kind of defect worth remembering.

### The sensitivity test was refitting while saying it was not

`_predict_at()` built and fitted a fresh model on every call. Test 5 called it on both the
original and the perturbed table, so perturbing the inputs also **retrained** the regression, the
aging walk and the participation model on the perturbed data — while the test's own printed text
said the fit was held fixed. What it measured was two effects added together.

The consequence was not cosmetic. This session reported a pass-through **rising** from 0.45 to
0.65 with distance and flagged the rise as an unexplained anomaly, on the reasoning that a distant
forecast should revert further toward the league rather than lean harder on the trailing anchor.
With one fitted object per page and only the inputs changed, the pass-through **falls**: 0.23 at
the valuation season to 0.15 five seasons out. The anomaly was the bug. The flag is withdrawn,
recorded rather than deleted, because it was published.

Both versions are now run and reported, the refit one named as what it is. The deletion cases also
print how many subject-horizons came back without an answer — 4,764 and 4,752 of 41,496 — because
removing a season changes who is eligible and the comparison was quietly becoming a comparison of
two populations.

### The residual diagnostics used the wrong year's band

After a harness run, `banded.spread_` holds the last page's calibrator. Reports 5 and 6 used it to
scale misses from every page while describing it as the band each miss was given. A 2015 forecast
was handed a different scale and a different shape from a 2021 one.

Every page's calibrator is now kept. The star figures at five seasons out move from +0.44/+3.41 to
**+0.51/+3.64**, so the concern survives and strengthens. The report also now carries the sharper
number: **18.5% of played star seasons finish above the 95th percentile of the shape their own page
was fitted with**, where 5% is intended, and 11.6% for the twenty-two-and-unders.

Report 6 was also overstated. Calling 1.045 a measured 4.5% of in-sample optimism is wrong: each
page's fitted and realized misses are different mixtures of seasons and players, and the ratio
carries that difference as well as any overfitting. It is now reported page by page — 0.942 to
1.114 — and described as a spread of ratios rather than an estimate. No inflation is applied.

### The distribution's mean was not the point forecast, and every guard passed

This is the one worth keeping in mind. The scaled misses were never centred, and they average about
+0.10 because the shape is right-skewed. So the conditional distribution was `mu + sigma * E[Z]`,
not `mu`, and its own mean sat up to **0.23 wins above the forecast column printed beside it**.

The wrapper's whole promise is that it adds a band and moves nothing. It kept that promise about the
**columns** and broke it about the thing the columns stand for, which is worse, because nothing
downstream reads the column. The simulation averages drawn paths. It would have taken the shifted
number, every contract would have been revalued by an amount nobody put there, and the check named
"the wrapper changes nothing" would have gone on passing. The zero-spread identity could not see it
either: it replaces the shape with zeros, and an absent shape cannot be off centre.

Fixed by centring on the mean of the piecewise-linear quantile function that is actually
interpolated, not the plain sample mean of the misses — the two differ because linear interpolation
gives the extreme order statistics half the weight of the rest, and centring on the wrong one would
leave a small offset in the thing being used. The amount removed, +0.0968 on the last development
page, is now printed as what it is: the average miss the forecast makes on seasons that happened.

**Centring rather than moving the point forecast is a choice and it is recorded as one.** The
alternative reading is that the residual mean is a bias correction and the forecast should be
`mu + sigma * E[Z]`. That is a change to the forecast. It would move every score in the variant
register and it belongs in the forecast's own phase with its own test, not smuggled in through an
interval.

Check 22 integrates the quantile function the code actually returns and requires its mean to equal
the expectation the harness scores. It is written to fail **both** ways: a version that stops
centring fails it, and a shape that happened to be centred already cannot pass it by luck. Confirmed
by neutering the centring and watching it fire.

### And one stale number of mine

The report quoted the placebo at 29-39%, which was the figure from the run with no ages. On the full
table it is 44-68%. Corrected.

## What was softened rather than fixed

Two claims in the report went further than the evidence. That pooling the shape across horizons
"holds" is one sample's description, not a test, and is now written that way. That **most** of the
star under-coverage is the forecast does not follow from a median and a tail quantile: the
diagnostic conditions on the player having played, while the coverage table includes
non-participation and the probability attached to it, so a whole component of the gap sits outside
what was measured. What the evidence supports is that both a low centre and a thin upper tail are
present and both need work, with the centre first.
