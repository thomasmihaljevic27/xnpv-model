## 1. The "survival effect" was removing the discounting too

Yesterday's report subtracted production's `npv_total` from its `surplus_no_survival` and called

The output said so and it was missed. Removing a survival haircut cannot lower a value when the
value is nonnegative and everything else is held, and the table reported **negative** effects at

# Session 2026-09-17c — the walk-away option and the control years

The reconciliation review closed, so this is the first item of the remaining valuation work: the
seasons a club still owns when a contract ends.

## What was missing

A contract that expires into unrestricted free agency ends the relationship. One that expires with
the player still restricted does not — the club holds his rights until he is eligible, and can keep
him on a qualifying offer, a one-year offer the CBA sizes off his last salary. The simulator was
valuing those seasons at zero.

**252 of the 1,217 development contracts own at least one**, and they are short deals: 133 one-year,
86 two-year, 31 three-year. That is the same population where the reconciliation found the rebuild
sitting *below* production, and where production's own terminal value appears.

## Why it had to be a stopping rule

Control is a right, not an obligation, and walking away is final — there is no skipping a year and
re-qualifying later. So the value is the value of a stopping rule, and the whole question is what
the club is allowed to know when it decides. Four rules on the same draws:

| control yrs | n | take every year | decide in advance | decide as you go | knew the path |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 88 | −0.556 | 0.079 | **0.190** | 0.367 |
| 2 | 93 | −0.038 | 0.317 | **0.614** | 0.934 |
| 3 | 55 | 1.117 | 1.111 | **1.453** | 1.628 |
| all | 252 | 0.136 | 0.451 | **0.716** | 0.950 |

A club **forced** to take every control year loses money on the deals holding one or two. The same
seasons, with the right to decline, are worth +$0.19M and +$0.61M. Deciding as you go is worth
**$0.27M a contract** over deciding in advance, which is production's rule — and that gap is the
part of the asset a point valuation cannot reach, because a point valuation has one path and
nothing to decide.

On a one-year deal the right to keep the player afterwards (+$0.94M) is worth more than the season
the club just bought (−$0.11M).

## Two things I got wrong on the way

**The ceiling was not a ceiling.** I first wrote the perfect-foresight rule as "stop at the first
year that turns out negative." That is not what a club that knew the whole path would do, because
the right dies when it walks away: on a path worth +5, −1, +10 it has to sit through the bad year to
reach the good one, and taking all three collects 14 against the myopic 5. The real ceiling is the
best prefix, including the empty one — never below zero, never below any other rule. The assertion I
had written (*nothing beats the oracle*) was failing on real contracts, which is how the error
surfaced: the guard was right and the rule was wrong.

**My first guard was also wrong in the other direction.** I asserted that every rule allowing a
walk-away must come out non-negative. Only the hindsight rule must: a club deciding on an
expectation can tender a player who then disappoints. Two of the 252 do exactly that. The runner now
reports that count rather than asserting it away.

## The identification question, which is the real content here

The informed rule is the one that carries the claim, so it is the one that could quietly read the
future. It updates the forecast by the misses already realised on the path, through the persistence
the simulator already fitted — nothing new is estimated — and it integrates through the empirical
shape on a Gauss–Hermite grid rather than substituting the conditional mean into it, which would be
the club expecting the median. It conditions the chance he plays on whether he is in the league
now, using the participation chain's own transition rather than the unconditional marginal.

Tested, not argued: every season from the decision onward is redrawn and the club's expectation
comes back **bit for bit**, at four horizons. Scrambling the *past* moves it at all four, so the
rule is not vacuously ignoring everything.

## Housekeeping that came with it

- `rebuild_config.league_min_path` now dates the league minimum the way `cap_path` already dated the
  ceiling — only figures published before the signing, 3% beyond — and carries the published 2026-29
  schedule. A control year can sit years past what its own valuation date knew.
- The offer bands are reimplemented in this tree rather than imported from production, then checked
  against production's implementation on 4,000 random cases: **largest difference $0.00**. Finding
  that check pass took fixing the minimum schedule first; it failed by $131,562 at first, which was
  the rebuild's table stopping at 2025 where production's runs to 2029.
- `exit_schedule` is factored out of `participation_path` rather than copied, and `per_season` is now
  a thin call on a `forecast_blocks` that answers for whatever seasons are asked. The review scripts
  read a four-element shape and still do.

## The approximation I could not remove

The offer formula runs off the final year's **base salary**. This tree's source carries one row per
contract with an average. Measured against the season spine: 421 of 593 agree within 1%, **155 have
a final salary above the average**, 90th percentile ratio 1.10. A front-loaded deal's last salary
sits above its average, so using the average makes the offer too cheap and the control year too
valuable — and it switches off exactly the 120%-of-cap-hit clause that exists to catch front-loading.
The direction is stated in the report rather than being left for someone to find.

# Session 2026-09-17d — the control-year pass, repaired

The review found four things wrong with yesterday's control-year build. All four are real, all four
are now fixed, and the headline figure changes by a factor of four.

## 1. A future decision was removing the right

The export's expiry status says what the club eventually did. "UFA no QO" is a club that declined to
qualify the player — and it declines *years after* the signing this values. My first pass read that
column and gave those 101 contracts no control years at all. That is the model being handed the
answer to the question it is asking: whether to walk away is precisely what the stopping rule
exists to decide. A further 45 were dropped on a plain "UFA" label while the export listed an
eligibility year after their own expiry.

Ownership is now eligibility and nothing else. `control_span` does not take the label as an
argument, and the suite asserts that on the function's signature so it cannot come back quietly.
The sample goes from **252 contracts to 398**.

Eligibility deserved an audit rather than a substitution, because it has two routes. A player is
unrestricted at 27 whatever else happens, which his birthdate fixes and a club knows on the day —
but seven accrued seasons can get him there sooner, and a season accrues by being played. So for a
player short of seven at the signing, part of that route runs through seasons that had not happened.

| | | |
|---|---:|---|
| export agrees with the age rule exactly | 1,087 | 89.3% |
| earlier (accrued seasons) | 130 | |
| later | 0 | |

Never later, which is what the rule says. And rather than describe the exposure I priced the whole
sample again on the age rule alone, which needs nothing but a birthdate: **+$0.026M a contract**,
under 4% of the answer.

## 2. The club was seeing shocks from seasons the player missed

This is the sharpest of the four. My conditioning ran over every earlier season's miss — including
seasons the player spent out of the league. Those misses exist inside the simulator, but nobody ever
observed them: watching a player miss a year tells you he missed it, not how well he would have
played.

The review measured it by changing only those hidden shocks: the first control year's decision moved
on **188 of 252 contracts**, and 26,519 path decisions flipped.

My future-scramble test could not have caught this, and that is the lesson worth keeping. The test
asked "does the rule read the future?" The defect was in the past — in the difference between what
happened and what was *seen* to happen. A leakage test is only as good as the observation model it
is written against, and I had not written one down.

The rule now conditions on the misses of played seasons and integrates the rest out. That is exact
rather than approximate: the joint law is Gaussian and the participation draws are independent of
it, so whether he played carries no information about how well he would have played, and dropping
those coordinates is the right thing. Paths are grouped by their observation pattern so the linear
solve runs once per pattern.

It also needed one thing stated that I had left implicit: conditioning on the miss is the same as
conditioning on the production a club actually watched only because the production shape is
monotone. `shape_is_monotone` asserts it.

## 3. A historical valuation was using future offer bands

I dated the league minimum and left the *bands* undated. The agreement carrying the 2026 bands was
ratified in the summer of 2025, so a contract signed in 2021 pricing a 2026 control year was being
handed a $1.10M offer where the rules it could see gave $1.00M. Dated now, the same way the cap
ceiling has been dated all along.

Half a fix is its own failure mode: I had the principle and applied it to one of the two inputs.

## 4. The headline compared three changes at once

The one that changes the number. My "informed" rule stopped at the first year whose *expected*
surplus went negative — the same myopia I had just finished removing from the ceiling, left in the
policy. And it averaged prices across uncertain production while the rule I compared it against
priced the mean; with the league-minimum floor bending the price, those two differ even when no
information has arrived.

So $0.27M was a mixture of pricing, policy and information. Six rules now sit on the same draws,
each differing from its neighbour in one thing, $M a contract over 398:

| take every year | production's rule | decide in advance | myopic informed | decide as you go | knew the path |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.285 | 0.443 | 0.637 | 0.704 | 0.704 | 0.904 |

| the comparison | what changes | $M |
|---|---|---:|
| as you go vs in advance | information only | **+0.068** |
| as you go vs myopic | counting later years only | +0.001 |
| the right vs the obligation | being able to walk away | **+0.419** |
| as you go vs production's rule | all three at once | +0.261 |

**The value of information is $0.068M, not $0.27M.** Counting the later years is worth almost
nothing on average — it changes 140 of 398 contracts but by $2,378 where it does — because the offer
escalates and a control year that disappoints is usually followed by a worse one. The constructed
+5, −1, +10 case is real; this sample rarely presents it.

And the largest number is not about information at all. **Being able to walk away is worth $0.419M
a contract**, six times what the club's information is worth. That is the finding worth carrying,
and it was sitting in the first version's table unremarked while I wrote up the wrong column.

## What this turned up about production

Production's `rfa_terminal_value.py` reads the same expiry column the same way — its docstring says
so plainly: "UFA no QO (team already declined to qualify) -> terminal value = 0". Of the 187
contracts where production carries no terminal value while this tree finds a right, **130 carry that
label**. So production's control-year value uses the outcome of the decision it is pricing, and any
back-test that leans on it has to carry that. Flagged.
