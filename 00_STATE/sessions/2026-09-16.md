# 2026-09-16: taking the coverage gap apart, and finding the participation model in it

The uncertainty repairs were verified clean the night before: all three findings closed, 22
checks passing with no skips, and the reviewer's own adversarial test confirming the new
distribution-mean guard fires when the fix is disabled. What was left was not a bug. It was the
open model question both reviews had now asked in the same words:

> The current evidence does not cleanly separate how much comes from forecasts being too low,
> uncertainty ranges being too narrow, or participation estimates.

That is a well-posed question, and it is also the first item on the queue, so it was the work.

## How it was asked

Three levers, each set to the value the outcomes say it should have had for that subgroup at that
horizon, coverage recomputed with each one alone and with all three: shift the centre until the
group's standardised misses sit where the band's shape says, stretch the band until its realized
span matches, replace the predicted probability of playing with the share who did.

Every one of them uses the answer. That is the point and it is stated three times in the file: it
measures the **ceiling** a perfect repair could reach with hindsight on this sample, which is an
upper bound and not a forecast of a real repair. A lever that cannot close the gap with hindsight
certainly cannot close it without.

Because an oracle says how much a lever could buy and not what is wrong with it, two things were
also measured with no hindsight at all: the participation probability against the realized play
rate, and the season-total bias split into rate, games and participation.

## What came back

**No single lever closes the star gap.** Five seasons out the best of the three closes 36% of the
distance to the stated 80%, and all three together overshoot it. Which one dominates changes with
distance: the centre near the valuation season, the spread and participation further out. The
star gap is three defects, not one, and I had been writing about it as one.

**For players 22 and under the centre is the wrong lever.** Correcting it makes their coverage
*worse* at five of six horizons. The spread carries 47 to 110% and participation 31 to 62%. The
oracle shift for them is near zero at the short horizons, so the forecast is not systematically
low for them where the gap opens. The queue's stated fix for this population — a prior from the
prospect pillar — addresses a component that is not the problem.

**Participation is squeezed toward the middle, and it is the finding of the day.** Predicted
against actual, one direction the whole length of both tables: 22-and-unders 0.677 against 0.804
five seasons out and under-predicted at *every* horizon; 3+ 0.781 against 0.883; 2-to-3 0.682
against 0.799; and the other way, 34-and-overs 0.491 against 0.373 at the valuation season. The
groups that survive are under-predicted and the groups that do not are over-predicted. That is
under-dispersion: the model shrinks every group toward the league.

**The aggregate could not see it.** Predicted 0.752 against 0.710 actual at the valuation season,
0.352 against 0.383 five out — the two halves of the error cancel — and the Brier score sits at
0.126 to 0.138 at every horizon with no trend. A single calibration number over a population
containing both halves of a compensating error is blind to it by construction. Worth remembering
the next time a flat metric is read as reassurance.

**The point bias agrees from the other side.** The stars' bias is almost entirely rate: −0.921 of
a −0.866 total five seasons out, with participation contributing +0.002 at three out. The
22-and-unders at three seasons out are the opposite: rate off by −0.072, total off by −0.340,
participation off by −0.114. A group whose total is badly biased while its rate is not has a
participation problem wearing a forecasting problem's clothes.

## What it changes

Participation moves to the front of the queue. It had been untouched Phase 2 work with no known
defect beyond the population question; it now has a measured, one-directional defect that reaches
both of the populations the model is worst at, and it is the only lever that helps every
under-covered group.

The star residual keeps its place and loses some scope. It is real, it is confirmed twice here,
it is a **rate** defect worth about nine tenths of a win a season five years out for the players
who carry the money — and it closes at most a quarter of the star coverage gap at that horizon.
It should not be sold as the whole of the star problem, which is how this branch was describing
it yesterday.

The band's own tier-or-age spread term comes off the deferred list. It had been parked until the
centre was fixed. For the young it is the largest single lever at every horizon and a centre
repair would not help them, so the deferral was waiting for nothing.

## Limits, said plainly

Every lever is an oracle. The star subgroup is 203 scored rows a horizon and 171 five out, with
far fewer careers behind them, so those figures are the least stable in the report. Levers
substitute for one another and a nonlinear interval does not decompose exactly into three causes,
which is why the overshoot column is printed. Coverage is not accuracy. Nothing in the document
is fitted for use and nothing is in the chain.

## Not touched

No production code. No locked decision. No reserved page. The confirmatory seal is intact.

## The fact check, and nine things I withdrew

Astra checked `c4c41f4` and reran the diagnostic. The tables reproduce. Most of what I concluded
from them does not, and two of the errors were contradicted by this file's own printed output
while I was writing the sentence.

### The levers were never ceilings

I called each adjustment a ceiling — the most a perfect repair could buy — and then reasoned that
since no single one closed the star gap, the gap must be three separate defects.

The adjustments are arbitrary. Align a median, match a middle-90 span, substitute a group mean.
None of them maximises coverage, and knowing the outcomes does not turn an arbitrary adjustment
into the best available one. Astra killed it in one line: **doubling the spread alone takes star
coverage five seasons out from 60.8% to 88.3%**, past everything I had reported as a bound. That
is not a proposal — coverage alone always rewards a wider band — but it is a counterexample, and
the bound and everything resting on it go with it. "Three defects, not one" and "the centre closes
at most a quarter" are withdrawn.

### The bias table was four units pretending to be one

I printed a season total in wins, a rate in wins per 82, a games error as a fraction of the
schedule and a participation error as a probability, and read them as contributions to the same
number. A probability error of −0.114 is not −0.114 wins.

The exact accounting, substituting each realized value in turn so the parts add up:

| group, three seasons out | total | participation | rate | games |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3+ | −0.647 | −0.004 | **−0.507** | −0.135 |
| 22 and under | −0.340 | −0.061 | −0.072 | **−0.207** |

My implementation reproduces the reviewer's figures to three decimals, which is the check that
mattered. **The young players' bias is mostly games played, not participation.** I had it wrong,
and the units error is exactly how.

The stars' rate finding survives unchanged.

**One thing came out of the correction that is worth keeping**: games played is the largest single
component of the young players' bias and the whole of the stars' bias at the valuation season, and
nothing in this rebuild has ever looked at it. It has been the quiet half of the rate-and-games
pair throughout.

### A comment that asserted a number I never measured

The diagnostic used the last page's residual shape for every page, under a comment saying the
pages differed by less than a hundredth at every quantile. I did not compute that. The largest
difference is 0.030, and the shortcut moved the star five-seasons-out baseline from its true 60.8%
to 60.2%. Each page now uses its own shape.

This is the project's own most-repeated rule — do not describe a mechanism you have not read, do
not state a figure you have not run — broken in the one place designed to make a shortcut look
checked.

### And four smaller ones

The participation error is not uniformly one-directional; stars are slightly over-predicted at the
short horizons and below-zero players under-predicted at the long ones. Participation does not
help every under-covered group; my own gap-closed table printed −16% and −4% for the 1-to-2 tier
while I wrote the sentence saying it did. A flat Brier score does not show subgroup errors
cancelling — a squared-error score does not cancel by sign; what cancels is mean predicted against
mean actual. And the star rows are 81 to 88 distinct careers, not the 171 to 203 rows I quoted.

All nine are listed in the report's section 5 rather than edited away, because the earlier version
was published and read.

## What survives, and it is the paragraph for the write-up

> The model has systematic forecast and interval errors for some age and ability groups,
> concentrated at longer horizons. Nominal 80% ranges hold 82-84% of outcomes overall but 61% for
> the highest trailing-ability group five seasons out and 66% for players aged 22 and under.
> Participation probabilities are miscalibrated for those same groups. The respective roles of
> participation, games played, the rate forecast and selection are unresolved.

Selection is a plausible contributor and is not identified. The harness already keeps departed
players as zero seasons rather than dropping them, and the aging curve already attempts a
correction, so this is not simply the error from testing on survivors. Identifying the mechanism
needs assumptions about who becomes observable, and a calibration table cannot supply them.

## The loop is closed

Thomas's call and the reviewer's recommendation agree, and they are right. Repeated adjustments to
one development sample that has been inspected many times cannot separate these causes, and each
pass spends credibility on a question the exercise is not built to answer.

The question that decides whether any of this matters is whether the thesis's own conclusions move:
do the valuations and the trade-mispricing categories survive reasonable forecast alternatives? If
they do, an imperfect forecast still supports the argument and the paragraph above is a
limitation. If they do not, that is a finding about the thesis rather than about calibration. That
is the next work, and the rest of the milestone follows it.

## Later: the question that decides whether any of it mattered

With the diagnostic loop closed, the work was the test both Thomas and the reviewer named: do the
valuations and the mispricing categories move when the forecast is varied — and how does the live
chain compare?

**First the environment.** The two production-adapter checks had been skipping for want of the
PuckPedia `.xlsx`, because production reads it with `read_excel` and only the CSV was here. The
CSV was read through the tree's own encoding-detecting reader and written to `.xlsx`; all 40
columns come back identical on both nulls and values. `20_CODE/age_join.py` then builds
`WAR_with_age.csv`, which is what the adapter needs. The suite runs **22 passed, 0 skipped, 0
failed** — the same as the reviewer's machine, so the live chain can now be priced here.

**The design.** Same 1,217 contracts, same cost side, same signing-dated rolling protocol, same
discounting and cap path, same seal. Five forecasts vary: the live chain, a trailing blend, the
calibrated total with and without participation, and the adopted candidate. Each gets its own
price line, because the currency is fitted on the forecast and a different forecast is a different
market. That makes the level incomparable across columns by construction and the gradient
comparable, which is the half the thesis rests on.

## What came back

**Three of five categories hold under every forecast, and the live chain agrees about all three.**
Below replacement is always negative, 0-to-0.5 always positive, and 1-to-2 wins always positive
and the largest solid category at +0.75 to +1.76 $M. Contract by contract the rebuilt forecasts
correlate 0.974 to 0.988 with the live chain, agree on sign for 88 to 97%, and differ by $0.24 to
$0.47M on deals worth millions. The two models are not describing different worlds.

**The top tier flips sign.** Live chain −0.19, trailing blend +1.05, the three rebuilt forecasts
−1.7 to −1.8. On **eighteen contracts**, from a sample that keeps only about half the six- and
eight-year deals.

That is the second independent reversal of the same headline. The currency-shape work already
found surplus at the top flipping between a straight and a log price line. This is a different
axis entirely and it flips it again. **The elite tier is not one design decision away from being
settled** — two defensible choices each reverse it, and the queue's standing instruction that no
back-test result about stars should be cited is now doubly evidenced.

**Removing the participation model changes almost nothing.** Top tier −1.68 against −1.74 with it,
correlation with the live chain 0.977 against 0.974. Two days of work went into characterising
that model's subgroup miscalibration, and on this evidence it does not move the valuation
ordering at all. Worth knowing before spending a phase on it; it is a reason to fix participation
for the forecast's own sake rather than for the thesis's.

**The term framing dwarfs every forecast choice.** Top-tier surplus swings $9M to $14M a contract
between term-in and term-free, against a $2.9M spread across all five forecasts. A framing
judgement is worth three to five times any modelling difference measured here. That ratio belongs
in the write-up, because it says plainly where the uncertainty in this model actually lives.

## Two things found in passing

Term coverage is 64% of eligible development contracts, and the loss is selective: about two
thirds of one-year deals survive against half the six- and eight-year ones. Long contracts are
where the top tier lives, so the thinnest part of the sample is thinned further by the mechanism
meant to protect it. Extending the forecast's reach is the cheapest real gain on the board.

`A2AgingParticipationImputed` raises an `AttributeError` on `fitted_horizons_` inside its own fit,
before any contract is priced, so a candidate the variant register carries as live cannot be run
through the contract path at all. Recorded, not repaired here.

## Where this leaves the thesis

The back-test can proceed on the three categories that survive. The elite category cannot be
cited, and the reason is no longer a calibration problem — it is eighteen contracts and two
framing choices. That is a better-posed problem than the one this week started with, and it is not
solved by a better forecast.

## The valuation review: my headline was an artifact of my own grouping

Astra reproduced the run and found three things. All three are right and every corrected figure I
computed matches theirs.

### The tiers were different populations

I cut each column's tiers from that column's own forecast, and I documented the choice — "a claim
about stars means the players that model calls stars." Then I read the spread across those columns
as the same elite contracts switching between bargain and overpay. They are not the same
contracts. The top tiers held **51, 68, 18, 19 and 18**, and I printed one n row.

Held to one declared grouping, cut on the candidate's forecast and joined by contract id:

| forecast | below 0 | 0 to 0.5 | 0.5 to 1 | 1 to 2 | 2+ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| production's forecast | −0.30 | +0.21 | +0.36 | +1.21 | −3.93 |
| trailing blend | −0.49 | +0.25 | +0.50 | +0.84 | −4.55 |
| calibrated + aging + participation | −0.42 | +0.10 | +0.63 | +1.76 | −1.74 |
| the adopted candidate | −0.41 | +0.10 | +0.56 | +1.66 | −1.80 |

**Every group keeps its sign in all five columns and the ordering is identical in all five.** No
reversal anywhere, including the 0.5-to-1 one I also reported. The corrected answer is the
stronger one, and I had talked myself out of it with a grouping choice.

What the columns really disagree about is **who the stars are**, which is a different and
interesting thing: 51 contracts against 18 for the same population. Contract by contract the
models correlate 0.974 to 0.988 and agree on sign for 88 to 97%.

The magnitude is still open — the top group runs −1.74 to −4.55 on 18 contracts with no
uncertainty estimate — and that is the honest residual.

### The participation test changed two things

I used `A1Calibrated3Aging` as the no-participation column, believing it isolated participation.
It also swaps the imputed survivorship aging for the uncorrected curve. The clean version pins the
probability of playing to one and holds everything else:

| fixed group | baseline | pinned, currency refitted | pinned, currency held |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2+ | −1.736 | −1.910 | −1.592 |

Signs and ordering survive, so the conclusion holds, but the movement is larger than the −1.74 to
−1.68 I reported and "removing participation barely matters" is withdrawn. So is the broader claim
that calibration errors are not what threatens the conclusion, which the confounded pair never
supported.

### I blamed the wrong stage for the missing contracts

I said the losses were long deals the forecast could not reach, and proposed extending its reach.
The attachment path already extrapolates past its fitted horizons. The accounting: 1,896 eligible,
438 lost at attachment, **241 lost because the signing quarter had too few earlier signings to fit
a price line**, 1,217 priced. Of 35 excluded six-year deals, 34 are lost at the price fit.
Extending the forecast restores none of them. I also read 842 rejections from the full
3,519-contract input as if they were development losses; only 438 are.

### And three smaller ones

The production column is production's forecast priced through **this tree's** currency, not the
production contract-NPV chain's output, and the adapter inherits production's full-panel aging
fit. Calling it "how the live chain compares" oversold it.

The "framing is worth three to five times any modelling difference" ratio had a denominator that
was itself a change of population. On the fixed top group the term contrast is $8.76M to $9.70M
across all five forecasts — large, real, and a contrast between two declared framings rather than
a measure of model uncertainty.

And the instruction I wrote into the queue — cite the three stable categories, drop the elite one
— is the selection problem this project exists to avoid. Withdrawn. The back-test evaluates the
predefined categories and reports where conclusions are sensitive.

## What the run now supports

A fixed set of contracts keeps its valuation ordering under five forecasts including production's
own, and under a participation alternative. That is a robustness check and it passed. It is not a
back-test, it is not evidence about performance, and it is not evidence of systematic trade
mispricing. Those three stay apart in the write-up.
