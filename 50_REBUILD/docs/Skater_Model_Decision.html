Two Skater Models

The current production chain and the rebuilt model both turn a skater's past seasons into a contract value in dollars. They take the same seven steps in different ways. This page shows each step side by side, what was wrong with the current version, what the rebuild does instead, and how the two score on the same seasons and contracts.

Where the decision stands (24 September 2026) The rebuilt skater model is closed at its final validation stage, with the limitations listed below. The choice between the two is deferred until the draft-pick and prospect models are taken up again, so that each asset class moves onto one currency together. Nothing has been merged into production.

The two chains, step by step

What was wrong, and what replaced it

Each description is taken from the script that implements it: 20_CODE/skater_forward_projection.py, aging_curve.py, and exit_hazard.py for the current model; 50_REBUILD/code/ for the rebuild. Figures are run outputs from the development pages (2015 to 2021).

1

What each model can see at the decision

Current model

The aging curve and the exit table are each fitted once on the full panel and then applied to valuations inside that panel; for example, a 2019 valuation uses an aging shape partly learned from 2021 seasons. The price line dates a player's production at the contract's start year, not at the signing. The signing-date audit found 30% of that sample signed before those seasons were complete, and 58% of the three-win-plus players.

Rebuilt model

Each fit rolls forward. A valuation sees only seasons complete before the decision date and contracts signed on or before it, and a training pair counts only if its outcome season falls before the page. Four look-ahead tests show zero change when later data is altered.

Why it matters: look-ahead bias is the first identification question for the thesis. A back-test of trades cannot rest on values that knew how the seasons turned out.

2

Where the player is now

Current model

The starting level is the raw 60/40 blend of the last two seasons' WAR totals (10+ games each). The aging curve does pull toward a comparables norm, but the projection divides the curve by its own first-season level, so the valuation season keeps the raw total. A great season is taken at face value. This is the source of the star over-forecast: three-win players are forecast 0.99 WAR too high in the valuation season.

Rebuilt model

A per-82 rate from three seasons with fitted weights, pulled toward the league by a regression fitted on the rolling window. The pull depends on how many games stand behind the number, and a second slope above one win keeps the best players from being pulled as hard. The three-win-plus bias in the valuation season is −0.17.

3

Aging

Current model

The raw total is multiplied by a ratio from the comparables curve. A ratio applied to a number near or below zero behaves badly, so the chain carries four guards: a floor on the curve's base, a cap and a floor on the ratio, and a flat fallback. A negative level is sent to replacement after the first season by rule.

Rebuilt model

A yearly change in the rate, by age, position, and the prior season's level, is added one season at a time. No guards are needed, and a negative level ages like any other. When the curve is fitted, players who left the league are put back at replacement level, so the curve is not fitted on survivors only.

4

Games played

Current model

The chain forecasts a season total directly, so availability and ability are one number. An injury-shortened season reads as a lower level, and there is no separate forecast of how much a player will play.

Rebuilt model

The share of games played is forecast on its own, from the trailing share, position, experience, and age, and multiplied onto a per-82 rate.

5

Is the player still in the league?

Current model

A separate exit table, by quality bucket and age group, multiplies each future season's value by the chance of still being around. The first missed season ends the career in that chain, and the table was fitted apart from an aging curve built only on players who kept playing. Its most confident fifth of predictions overstates playing by 25 points (18 on the rows it can answer).

Rebuilt model

The probability of a 10+ game season is forecast for each future season, so a player can miss a year and come back. It reads the contracts signed by the decision date. Chance × rate × games share is formed once. Participation Brier score: 0.136 against 0.205 on the same rows.

6

The price of a win

Current model

A line of cap share on trailing realised WAR, censored at the league minimum, with a steeper slope for defencemen. It is dated by contract start (see step 1), and each season is priced separately against a cap that grows 3% a year.

Rebuilt model

A censored line on forecasts dated at the signing, refitted each signing quarter on the contracts signed before it. Term is part of the price, and restricted and unrestricted free agents share one line with their rights entering as features.

7

The range of outcomes

Current model

A normal spread from the script's own backcast (0.52, 0.74, and 0.80 WAR average miss one to three seasons out), held flat past three seasons as a placeholder and set to zero in the valuation season. It is used only to correct the value near the league-minimum floor; no contract gets a distribution.

Rebuilt model

2,000 careers are drawn for each contract. A miss carries into later seasons at a fitted rate, and an exit sticks. Each path is priced and the prices averaged, so each contract has a full distribution. The pooled calibration tests do not detect miscalibration; that is a pooled result, not a guarantee for each contract.

How they score

Same pages, same seasons, and one price line for both models' dollars. What the rebuild does not fix

  • Stars at long horizons. For three-win-plus players the rebuilt forecast runs 0.42 WAR low one season out, 0.64 three out, and 0.87 five out. The miss sits in how the aging walk carries the rate forward. Eight repair attempts were scored and none was adopted; it is recorded as a limitation.
  • Dollars. The forecast gain shows up in absolute error but is not decisive on squared error, where a few long, expensive contracts dominate. The rebuilt values also run lower on average than realised production.
  • One vendor assumption. Contract status in the participation forecast assumes the vendor's contract snapshot is complete for deals ending from 2018.
  • Long contracts. Production values eight-year deals at −$28.2M on average; the rebuilt surplus on the same deals is $33.8M higher. How much of that gap is the term-in price line and how much is forecasting has not been separated.
  • No clean holdout. The 2022 to 2025 pages were examined by about thirty variants before the rebuild, so a final run on them is a check, not an independent test.

The choice, when it is made

Recommended

Carry the rebuilt model forward

Better season forecasts on the current model's own rows, each input dated at the decision, and a distribution for each contract. The cost is the star under-forecast at long horizons and a more negative dollar bias.

Alternative

Keep the current model

Closer on average in dollars, but through offsetting misses: it over-forecasts each tier from one win up. It also carries the full-panel fits and start-year dating described in step 1.

Sensitivity

Rebuilt, without contract data

The same model with participation that ignores contract status, which removes the vendor assumption. It is worse on squared error (1,999 of 2,000 contract resamples), slightly better on dollar absolute error, and its middle 50% range holds 5.4 points too few outcomes.
